This post demonstrates a surprising finding about policy evaluation using deep networks: direct supervised learning of the value function is worse than TD learning *at its own objective*.

Let's dig into each component of this claim, and then take a look at the evidence.
## Background
Every policy $\pi$ has its own state-value function, $V^\pi$. *Policy Evaluation* refers to the estimation of $V^\pi$. It is a key component of all deep RL algorithms. An example is the training of the critic in PPO. Let's mathematically define policy evaluation. 

The goal of policy evaluation is to construct an estimate $v \approx V^\pi$ to minimize the mean-squared value error over a weighing distribution $\mu$.
$$\overline{VE} = \mathbb{E}_{\mu(s)}(v(s) - V^\pi(s))^2$$

The target, $V^\pi$ can be defined as the following length $N$-vector:
$$V^\pi  = \sum_{t\geq 0} (\gamma P^\pi)^t R^\pi$$
where $P^\pi(s,s') = \sum_a \pi(a|s) P(s'|s,a)$ and $R^\pi(s) = \sum_a \pi(a|s) R(s,a)$ are the state-level transition and reward associated with this policy.

The choice of weighting distribution $\mu$ is an open question (see my paper on Approximate Next Policy Sampling[link] for a discussion of this). In this post, we take $\mu$ to be the on-policy distribution, which can be sampled by rolling out $\pi$. Formally, $\mu_\gamma(s) = (1-\gamma) \mu_0^\intercal \sum_{t\geq 0} (\gamma P^\pi)^t$, where $\mu_0$ is the start state distribution. I'll use $\mu$ to refer to the un-discounted state distribution $\lim_{\gamma \rightarrow{} 1} \mu_\gamma$. 

With this choice of $\mu$, minimizing $\overline{VE}$ becomes *on-policy policy evaluation*, because the state value errors are weighted by exactly how frequently they appear in the training data generated by $\pi$. In the next section, I discuss two forward algorithms to minimize $\overline{VE}$. The first is a noise free version of Monte Carlo, and the second is noise-free TD.
## Algorithms
The first algorithm is **Supervised Value Learning**. It's exactly what it sounds like: *given access* to $V^\pi$, we can use Adam or our favorite optimizer to fit $v_\theta$ to it using regression.  I run the following algorithm:

**Algorithm 1: Supervised Value Learning (SVL)**
Given $\pi$ and $V^\pi$
- Initialize network $v_\theta$
- Repeatedly update $\theta$ by minimizing $\overline{VE}(\theta)$ with Adam

Note that SVL is completely deterministic, excluding the initialization of $\theta$. Since it is given direct access to $V^\pi$, it does not suffer from the well-known variance of Monte Carlo estimation. Thus, one might expect SVL to achieve skyline performance: e.g. the best possible performance that we can obtain with our network architecture. Indeed, this is guaranteed when $v_\theta$ is linear. In that case, SVL is standard linear regression.

Next, I define the second algorithm: Expected TD Learning. Typically, TD learning samples states. In this post, I take a look at what happens in expectation, eliminating the randomness due to sampling. 

**Algorithm 2: Expected TD**:
Given fixed policy $\pi$, $P^\pi$ and $R^\pi$
- Initialize network $v_\theta$
- Repeat:
	-  Compute $Tv_\theta(s) = R^\pi(s) + \gamma P^\pi v_\theta(s)$ at every state
	- $L_{TD} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_s \mu(s) (\text{stop-grad}(Tv_\theta) - v_\theta(s))^2$ 
	- Update $\theta$ by minimizing $L_{TD}$ with Adam

### Reason 1: More Uniform Value Error
The distribution of value errors at iteration 1000 (when both have similar $\overline{VE}$), demonstrates one possible reason for this. Observe that SVL obtains errors that are concentrated in the goal room. This is expected, since the random policy rarely reaches the goal room. On the other hand, TD has fairly uniform value errors across the state space.

Why might this be? Early in learning, the TD error is approximately zero, except at the goal state. That means, early in learning, the network capacity is focused on fitting the goal state and its neighbors, despite their low weight under $\mu$. 

![[td_vs_mc_value_errors.pdf]]
Of course, fitting the value function uniformly won't minimize our objective. In some sense, SVL is doing the right thing. However, after using most of its capacity in the earlier rooms, it may reach a local optimum where no more capacity can be put into fitting the last room.

## Experiment 2: Fixed Policy on Four Rooms
In the prior example, the Markov Chain induced by the policy  was strongly connected: all states can reach all other states. Moreover, it had the (somewhat) unique property that the policy rarely reached the terminal state. This is characteristic of early learning, or a challenging exploration task, where fitting the higher values is important for policy improvement, even if they are rare under the on-policy distribution.


### Reason 2: Capacity Preservation

For a better, more qualitative understanding, I look at the network's representation learning dynamics via the Jacobian matrix ($J[s] = \nabla_\theta v(s)^\top$).

The Jacobian spectrum (Figure 3) reveals a dramatic difference in how capacity is used. At initialization, both networks start with a low-rank, compressed spectrum. By the end of training:

- **TD maintains a rich, high-capacity spectrum:** The singular values stay high and flat across all dimensions. 
    
- **SVL suffers from spectral decay:** SVL raises its singular values, but its spectrum drops off sharply. 

TD's bootstrapping forces the network to utilize a diverse set of feature dimensions to satisfy Bellman consistency across the entire transition graph. In contrast SVL, concentrates all its representational power on the most frequently visited states.

This capacity starvation is further visible in the centered eNTK (Pearson correlation) matrices. SVL learns broad, complex cross-room correlations dictated by the stationary distribution weighting. In contrast, TD's centered correlations are cleaner and more localized. This near-diagonal structure shows that TD’s bootstrapping acts as a structural regularizer: it forces the network to respect local graph topology and propagate updates smoothly along the MDP edges, rather than wasting capacity trying to globally fit distant regions based solely on how often they are visited.

  

**In short:** SVL’s regression objective allows the network to neglect rare states, collapsing its effective Jacobian rank and sacrificing accuracy at the goal. TD’s bootstrapping acts as a dense curriculum that forces the network to maintain a rich, high-rank representation across the entire state space.

I perform some analysis of the representation learning dynamics. The high-level finding is that the TD learning representation has highly correlated gradients: an update to state $s$ brings all other states in a similar direction. However, despite this, TD maintains a higher rank representation, meaning that it generalizes but is still able to move each state individually.

The first piece of evidence is the spectra (singular values) of the Jacobian (matrix of gradients). The Jacobian $J$ is a $|S| \times |\theta|$ matrix, where row $s$ is $\nabla_\theta v(s)^\intercal$. Its maximum rank is the size of the state-space (104). Figure 3 shows the spectra for TD and SVL (labelled as MC). The dotted lines denote the start of learning, and the solid line denotes the end. We can see that TD's first singular value is almost 100, but then it quickly decays to have most singular values near 1. In contrast, MC decay's more rapidly.

**Figure 3: Four Rooms Jacobian Spectra**
![[jacobian_svals.pdf]]

For fun, we can also take a look at the associated singular vectors. SVL splits responsibility for the fourth room between the first two singular vectors. The start room is captured by the first singular value. Finally, it's fourth and fifth seem to be random coverage of the other rooms.
![[mc_jacobian_svectors.pdf]]

In contrast, TD learning's singular values have more structure. The first is the entire state space, the second is the goal room, and later ones are clusters in state space. 
![[td_jacobian_svectors.pdf]]


More evidence that this representation learning dynamic occurs I look at the empirical neural tangent kernel (eNTK), an $|S| \times |S|$ diagonal matrix where each entry$(s, \tilde{s}) = \text{cosine-similarity}(\nabla v_\theta(s), \nabla v_\theta(\tilde{s}))$. An entry equals 1 when an update to $v_\theta(s)$ moves $v_\theta(s')$ in the same direction, and is -1 when they move in the opposite direction. Tabular RL would have no generalization, and therefore have the identity matrix for its eNTK. Meanwhile SVL results in a block structure, with positive correlation between some states. TD results in all elements being very near one. This just says that raising the value at one state raises the value at all states - which is expected behavior for TD.

More interesting, when I center the gradients by subtracting the mean, before computing the cosine similarity, we see that "underneath" the smearing, the eNTK for TD has a near-diagonal structure. This implies TD is doing a relatively unsophisticated form of generalization compared to MC, which could make sense since the underlying MDP is a fully-connected random walk.

![[Uncentered TD vs MC.pdf]]

![[Centered TD vs MC.pdf]]

So in short, the first order approximation of the TD update is to update the whole state space, but the higher order terms are still existant.
